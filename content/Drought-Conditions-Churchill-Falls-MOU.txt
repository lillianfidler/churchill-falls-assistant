Drought Conditions, Imports, and Reservoir Management

In recent years, Hydro-Québec's quarterly reporting has increasingly reflected concerns associated with drought conditions, reduced reservoir inflows, and the operational impacts of lower water availability across Québec's hydroelectric system. While the corporation does not generally characterize these developments as a direct threat to its long-term strategy, the reports nevertheless reveal growing emphasis on reservoir management, imports, system flexibility, and climate-related operational pressures.

One of the most notable developments in recent reporting has been Hydro-Québec's increasingly explicit reference to reduced runoff conditions and lower hydraulic inflows affecting major reservoirs. These conditions have required the corporation to increase electricity imports from neighboring jurisdictions in order to preserve reservoir storage levels and maintain system reliability. Quarterly reporting issued during 2025 showed a substantial increase in imported electricity compared with previous years, suggesting that Hydro-Québec has become more reliant on external markets during periods of reduced water availability.

The 2025 Annual Report reinforced this trend by acknowledging that Hydro-Québec has been operating within a context of prolonged lower hydraulic inflows since approximately 2023. The report places strong emphasis on the management of the corporation's reservoirs as strategic energy-storage assets capable of supporting market optimization and long-term reserve management. The reporting also highlights increased focus on preserving operational flexibility through imports, demand management, and transmission-system optimization.

The reports suggest that Hydro-Québec is using imports strategically as part of a broader reservoir-management approach. Because Québec's hydroelectric system possesses significant storage capacity, Hydro-Québec can conserve water during lower inflow periods by purchasing electricity from external markets when conditions are favorable, while continuing to export electricity during periods of stronger market demand. This operational flexibility has historically represented one of Hydro-Québec's major advantages within northeastern North American electricity markets.

At the same time, increased reliance on imports raises important questions regarding the long-term sustainability of Hydro-Québec's traditional operating model under changing climate conditions. Multi-year drought patterns and declining reservoir replenishment levels could potentially reduce the availability of surplus hydroelectricity for export markets, particularly during winter peak-demand periods. Since export revenues remain an important contributor to Hydro-Québec's financial performance, prolonged low-water conditions could eventually affect both revenue stability and long-term planning assumptions.

Evolution of Hydro-Québec's Reporting on Drought and Low Hydraulicity

References to drought conditions and reduced hydraulic inflows began to appear more noticeably within Hydro-Québec's public reporting during 2023, although the issue was initially framed primarily as a temporary operational and market-related challenge rather than a long-term strategic concern. Early references focused mainly on lower export revenues, reduced water inflows, and weaker external electricity sales associated with lower-than-normal runoff conditions. At this stage, Hydro-Québec's reporting generally presented the issue as part of the normal variability historically experienced within large hydroelectric systems rather than as evidence of structural climate-related change.

A more significant shift became visible during 2024. Quarterly reporting issued throughout 2024 increasingly referred to ongoing low runoff conditions, below-normal precipitation, reduced snow cover, and the need for prudent reservoir management. The language also became more repetitive and consistent across successive reporting periods, suggesting that lower hydraulic inflows were no longer being treated as an isolated or short-term event. Hydro-Québec began placing greater emphasis on:
• preserving reservoir storage levels,
• managing energy reserves,
• reducing certain export activities,
• optimizing electricity purchases and sales, and
• maintaining operational flexibility under prolonged low inflow conditions.

The tone of the reporting also evolved gradually over time. While Hydro-Québec consistently avoided alarmist language, the issue of hydrological variability became increasingly integrated into broader discussions of operational planning, financial performance, infrastructure investment, and long-term system management. By late 2024 and into 2025, references to prolonged low hydraulicity conditions suggested that the corporation increasingly viewed reduced water availability as an ongoing operating condition rather than a temporary anomaly.

Importantly, however, Hydro-Québec's reporting continued to emphasize resilience, optimization, flexibility, and prudent management rather than vulnerability or crisis. As a result, the urgency of the issue appears to have increased operationally without a corresponding increase in overtly urgent public language.

The evolution of Hydro-Québec's reporting may therefore reflect a gradual transition in operational and planning assumptions. Rather than presenting drought conditions as a temporary disruption, recent reporting increasingly suggests that hydrological uncertainty may be emerging as a recurring factor influencing reservoir management, infrastructure investment, supply diversification, and long-term electricity planning.


Strategic Implications for Action Plan 2035

The operational implications of prolonged drought conditions are already becoming visible within Hydro-Québec's reporting. In the short term, lower inflows appear to be affecting the corporation primarily through reservoir management, market activity, and electricity procurement. Hydro-Québec's reporting indicates that the corporation has responded to reduced inflows by managing reservoirs more conservatively, reducing some external sales, and increasing electricity purchases from neighboring markets.

These developments do not necessarily indicate an immediate reliability crisis. However, they do suggest a more constrained operating environment in which Hydro-Québec may need to rely more heavily upon imports and short-term market purchases in order to preserve reservoir levels while meeting domestic demand and maintaining export flexibility. The trend may also reduce Hydro-Québec's ability to maximize export revenues during certain periods, particularly if water reserves must increasingly be conserved for winter demand and long-term system security.

Churchill Falls MOU and the Evolving Strategic Context

The Churchill Falls Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) between Hydro-Québec and Newfoundland and Labrador Hydro may become increasingly important within the broader context of evolving hydrological conditions, electrification demand, and long-term supply planning. Although Hydro-Québec's reporting does not explicitly frame the agreement as a climate-adaptation measure, the timing and scale of the proposed Labrador developments appear broadly consistent with the corporation's growing emphasis on long-term supply resilience, diversification, and operational flexibility.

The agreement, announced in late 2024, establishes a framework for future hydroelectric development in Labrador, including the proposed Gull Island generating station, expansion of existing Churchill Falls facilities, upgrades to existing infrastructure, and major transmission investments. Collectively, these projects could eventually provide approximately 3,900 MW of additional renewable generating capacity.

In the short term, the Churchill Falls MOU does not directly resolve the operational challenges associated with lower inflows currently affecting Québec's hydroelectric system. However, the agreement may provide Hydro-Québec with greater long-term planning flexibility as the corporation attempts to balance rising electricity demand, electrification objectives, export commitments, and increasing hydrological uncertainty.

The broader significance of the MOU appears particularly important when viewed alongside the evolution of Hydro-Québec's reporting since approximately 2023. During this period, Hydro-Québec has increasingly emphasized:
• reservoir preservation and energy-reserve management,
• expanded transmission infrastructure,
• operational flexibility,
• interconnections,
• renewable-energy diversification, and
• long-term system resilience.

Within this evolving context, the Labrador developments may eventually provide an important supplementary source of renewable generation capable of supporting both domestic demand growth and export-market stability during periods of reduced reservoir inflows within Québec.

Importantly, however, the available evidence does not suggest that the Churchill Falls MOU should be interpreted as a direct response to drought conditions alone. Rather, the evolving hydrological and operational environment may be altering the broader strategic context within which the negotiations and future Labrador developments are occurring.

Historically, Hydro-Québec's system benefited from extensive reservoir storage and relatively stable long-term hydrological conditions. However, the increasing visibility of reduced runoff conditions, imports, reservoir-management pressures, and diversification initiatives suggests that long-term supply flexibility and geographic diversification may now hold greater strategic importance than under earlier planning assumptions.

Within this context, the Churchill Falls and Gull Island developments may increasingly be viewed not simply as economic-growth initiatives, but also as part of a broader evolution toward enhanced long-term supply resilience and diversified renewable generation under changing climate conditions.
