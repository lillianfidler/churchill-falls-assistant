ARE WE READY TO NEGOTIATE WITH HYDRO-QUÉBEC?

An Assessment of Whether Newfoundland and Labrador Appears Strategically Ready to
Negotiate a Long-Term Churchill River Agreement with Hydro-Québec

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This assessment examines whether Newfoundland and Labrador is currently institutionally and
strategically prepared to negotiate a long-term Churchill River agreement with Hydro-Québec
within an evolving North American electricity landscape.

The analysis draws upon earlier work involving:

• Hydro-Québec’s Strategy 2035 and quarterly reporting;

• the New England Clean Energy Connect (NECEC) project;

• transmission and interconnection strategy;

• climate variability and reservoir-management pressures;

• post-2041 Churchill Falls positioning;

• observations and recommendations arising from the Independent Review Committee (IRC);

• and the emerging Federal National Electricity Strategy.

The central conclusion is nuanced.

Newfoundland and Labrador appears substantially more aware of the strategic realities
surrounding Churchill River negotiations than in earlier decades. Various analyses, including the
IRC's work, suggest a growing recognition of important issues involving transmission access,
pricing asymmetries, long-term industrial development, governance, strategic flexibility, and
future development options.

However, the province still does not appear fully positioned institutionally or strategically for
negotiations with a counterpart as sophisticated, integrated, and strategically coordinated as
Hydro-Québec.

A major distinction emerges between:

• recognizing the strategic issues involved; and

• possessing a fully developed negotiating architecture capable of responding to an increasingly
complex electricity-system environment.

The observations arising from this work further suggest that Hydro-Québec increasingly operates not
simply as a traditional provincial utility, but as a continental-scale electricity-system participant
focused on:

• reliability;

• transmission integration;

• climate adaptation;

• export stability;

• and long-term strategic positioning.

By comparison, Newfoundland and Labrador still appears comparatively fragmented among:

• political objectives;

• fiscal pressures;

• operational realities within Newfoundland and Labrador Hydro;

• industrial-development aspirations;

• and broader long-term strategic planning.

The emerging Federal National Electricity Strategy could materially alter the future negotiating
environment by:

• encouraging greater interprovincial integration;

• supporting expanded transmission corridors;

• increasing national energy-security coordination;

• and potentially reframing Churchill Falls as a national strategic asset rather than solely a
bilateral Newfoundland and Labrador–Québec issue.

One of the strongest recurring conclusions throughout the assessment is that transmission
optionality may ultimately be the central strategic issue.

Hydro-Québec currently possesses:

• transmission scale;

• export access;

• operational flexibility;

• and broad market optionality.

Newfoundland and Labrador, while increasingly aware of the importance of these dynamics, still
appears to possess comparatively limited independent optionality and a less fully articulated
long-term electricity-system strategy.

The overall conclusion is therefore twofold:

Intellectually and analytically, Newfoundland and Labrador appears substantially more
prepared than in the past.

Institutionally and strategically, however, the province may still be developing the long-
term integrated framework required to negotiate effectively in an increasingly continental-
scale electricity and transmission environment.

ASSESSMENT OF WHETHER NEWFOUNDLAND AND LABRADOR APPEARS
STRATEGICALLY READY TO NEGOTIATE A LONG-TERM CHURCHILL RIVER
AGREEMENT WITH HYDRO-QUÉBEC

OVERVIEW

Based on work completed examining:

• Hydro-Québec’s quarterly reports and Strategy 2035;

• the New England Clean Energy Connect (NECEC) analysis;

• transmission and interconnection strategy;

• climate variability and reservoir-management pressures;

• post-2041 Churchill Falls positioning;

• observations and recommendations arising from the Independent Review Committee (IRC);

• and the emerging Federal National Electricity Strategy,

the conclusion would be:

Newfoundland and Labrador appears substantially more aware of the strategic issues involved in
Churchill River negotiations than in earlier decades.

A number of recent analyses, including the work of the IRC, demonstrate growing recognition of
important structural issues involving transmission access, pricing, industrial development,
governance, and future development options.

However, the province still does not appear fully positioned institutionally or strategically for
negotiations of the scale and complexity involved with Hydro-Québec.

There is an important distinction between:

• recognizing the strategic issues; and

• possessing a fully developed negotiating architecture capable of responding effectively within
an evolving electricity-system environment.

The various analyses reviewed move significantly toward recognizing these realities. However,
Newfoundland and Labrador’s broader institutional and strategic framework still appears only
partially developed relative to Hydro-Québec’s long-term planning sophistication and system
integration.

1. The Strategic Environment Appears to Be Changing

One of the most important observations emerging from this work is that the strategic
environment surrounding Churchill River development may be changing.

This observation emerges from analyses of Hydro-Québec's Strategy 2035, Hydro-Québec's
quarterly reports, the report Drought Conditions, Evolving Operational Context, and Potential
Implications for the Churchill Falls Memorandum of Understanding, the New England Clean
Energy Connect (NECEC) project, transmission and interconnection developments, observations
and recommendations arising from the Independent Review Committee (IRC), and consideration
of the emerging Federal National Electricity Strategy.

Taken together, these analyses suggest future electricity systems may place increasing
importance on:

• transmission access;

• interconnection;

• reliability;

• flexibility;

• climate adaptation;

• optionality;

• and long-term system integration.

If so, Churchill Falls may increasingly need to be viewed within a broader strategic context than
has often been the case historically.

The issue may no longer be simply one of electricity pricing.

Rather, the observations emerging from this work suggest the value of large hydroelectric resources may
increasingly be linked to how they contribute to larger electricity systems that must balance reliability,
changing demand patterns, climate variability, and expanding electrification.Much of this thinking
emerged from earlier examinations of Hydro-Québec's Strategy 2035, Hydro-Québec quarterly
reporting, and the NECEC project. Observations and recommendations arising from the IRC
appear, in a number of areas, to identify similar strategic considerations.

If the strategic environment is changing in this manner, the capabilities required for successful
negotiations may also be changing.

A. Transmission May Be Emerging as a Strategic Asset

One of the clearest observations arising from the NECEC and transmission analyses is that
transmission access itself appears to be increasingly important.

Historically, discussions surrounding Churchill Falls often focused primarily on electricity
generation and pricing.

The analyses reviewed suggest that transmission access increasingly influences:

• bargaining power;

• market access;

• pricing opportunities;

• system flexibility;

• reliability;

• and long-term strategic options.

Hydro-Québec's strength appears to derive not only from generation assets, but also from
extensive transmission infrastructure and access to multiple electricity markets.

Observations and recommendations arising from the IRC similarly highlighted concerns
regarding transmission access, dependence upon Hydro-Québec infrastructure, and the
limitations such dependence may create.

Taken together, these observations suggest transmission should perhaps be viewed not simply as
supporting infrastructure, but as a strategic asset capable of influencing future development
opportunities.

This represents a significant shift from earlier discussions that focused primarily on electricity
pricing.

B. Hydro-Québec Appears to Be Operating Increasingly at a System Level

Another important observation emerging from this work is that Hydro-Québec increasingly
appears to be operating within a broader electricity-system framework.

Strategy 2035, quarterly reporting, export positioning, climate-related pressures, and
interconnection developments suggest an organization focused on:

• reliability;

• flexibility;

• transmission integration;

• export stability;

• climate adaptation;

• and long-term strategic positioning.

The analysis suggests Churchill Falls may be valuable within such a framework not solely
because it produces electricity, but because it contributes to:

• winter reliability;

• reservoir-management flexibility;

• export stability;

• system resilience;

• and broader decarbonization objectives.

Observations and recommendations arising from the IRC appear broadly consistent with this
interpretation.

This represents a more sophisticated interpretation than the traditional assumption that Hydro-
Québec's primary interest is simply obtaining access to low-cost electricity.

C. The Financial versus Economic Development Question Remains Important

One of the strongest themes emerging from this work is the distinction between financial returns
and broader economic development.

Newfoundland and Labrador has long faced questions regarding the extent to which Churchill
River development should focus upon:

• maximizing electricity-export revenues; or

• supporting broader provincial economic transformation.

This issue emerged repeatedly throughout earlier analyses and was also reflected in observations
and recommendations arising from the IRC.

The distinction is important because the answer may influence future decisions regarding:

• industrial development;

• transmission priorities;

• export strategies;

• infrastructure investments;

• and negotiation objectives.

Viewed in this way, Churchill Falls increasingly appears to be more than an electricity-pricing
issue.

It may also represent a broader economic-development and public-policy question.

2. Implications for Newfoundland and Labrador

If the strategic environment surrounding Churchill River development is changing, an important
question follows:

Is Newfoundland and Labrador currently positioned to negotiate effectively within that
environment?

One of the central observations emerging from this work is that a number of important strategic issues
have now been brought more clearly into focus within discussions surrounding Churchill River
development.

Observations and recommendations arising from the Independent Review Committee (IRC)
contributed to this discussion. Similar themes also emerged from analyses involving Hydro-
Québec's Strategy 2035, Hydro-Québec quarterly reporting, the New England Clean Energy
Connect (NECEC) project, transmission developments, climate variability, and the emerging
Federal National Electricity Strategy.

Taken together, these analyses point toward a number of issues that may become increasingly
important in future negotiations and development decisions.

However, identifying important issues is not the same as possessing the capabilities required to
respond to them.

This distinction may be one of the most important observations emerging from the work
completed thus far.

Hydro-Québec appears to operate within a highly integrated strategic framework of the type discussed
earlier in this assessment.

The question is whether Newfoundland and Labrador currently possesses a comparable
framework.

The analyses reviewed do not appear to demonstrate the existence of a similarly integrated long-
term strategic architecture.

Nor do they appear to demonstrate the existence of a clearly articulated framework linking future
Churchill River development, transmission strategy, industrial development, market
participation, and broader provincial objectives.

This does not mean such capabilities cannot be developed.

Nor does it imply that important work has not been undertaken.

Rather, the question is whether the strategic framework required to address the issues identified
throughout this assessment presently exists in a sufficiently developed form.

This question becomes increasingly important if the strategic environment itself is evolving, as
seems apparent.

If future negotiations involve more than electricity pricing alone, then the capabilities required
for successful negotiations may also extend beyond traditional negotiating considerations.

The key question therefore is not whether important issues have been identified.

The key question is whether Newfoundland and Labrador currently possesses the strategic
architecture required to respond effectively to them.

3. Newfoundland and Labrador Has Often Appeared More Reactive Than Proactive

Hydro-Québec entered recent discussions with Strategy 2035, defined supply-security objectives,
transmission planning, export-market objectives, climate-adaptation considerations, and long-
term institutional goals.

The analyses reviewed suggest a high degree of integration between these various elements.

By comparison, Newfoundland and Labrador appears to be responding to developments more than
operating from a fully articulated long-term development blueprint.

The issue is not whether important questions have been identified.

Many have.

Nor is the issue whether useful contributions have been made.

The IRC, together with the broader analyses reviewed, has contributed to identifying a number of
issues that may warrant consideration as future decisions are made.

The broader question is whether Newfoundland and Labrador currently possesses a sufficiently
developed strategic framework capable of guiding future Churchill River negotiations and
development.

The analyses reviewed suggest this remains an open question.

This observation is significant because many of the issues identified throughout this assessment
involve long-term strategic choices rather than conventional negotiating questions.

They involve questions of future positioning, future development pathways, institutional
capability, strategic flexibility, and long-term objectives.

Taken together, the analyses suggest future success may depend not simply upon negotiating
skill, but upon the existence of a broader strategic framework within which negotiations occur.

This may ultimately be one of the most important lessons emerging from the work completed
thus far.

4. The Emerging Federal National Electricity Strategy Could Alter the Strategic
Environment

The emerging Federal National Electricity Strategy may become one of the most important long-
term developments affecting Churchill Falls.

The emerging Federal National Electricity Strategy appears to envision a future involving:

• a more integrated national electricity system;

• expanded interprovincial interconnections;

• strategic transmission corridors;

• and broader energy-security coordination.

If this occurs, the strategic environment changes materially.

Churchill Falls may increasingly become:

• a national strategic asset;

rather than merely:

• a bilateral Newfoundland and Labrador–Québec issue.

This could potentially:

• reduce Newfoundland and Labrador's isolation;

• alter transmission politics;

• create federal financing opportunities;

• and reshape bargaining leverage over time.

One of the more important observations emerging from analyses of Hydro-Québec's Strategy
2035, Hydro-Québec quarterly reporting, transmission developments, and the New England
Clean Energy Connect (NECEC) project is that electricity systems may be becoming
increasingly interconnected and strategically integrated.

The Federal National Electricity Strategy may represent an early indication of this broader trend.

If so, future Churchill River development may need to be considered within a wider national
context than has traditionally been the case.

The analyses reviewed suggest this possibility warrants consideration because it may influence
future decisions involving:

• transmission development;

• market access;

• energy security;

• industrial development;

• infrastructure investment;

• and long-term strategic positioning.

The implications remain uncertain.

However, if Canada gradually moves toward greater electricity-system integration, the strategic
environment within which future Churchill River negotiations occur may be significantly
different from the one that existed historically.

This possibility does not eliminate the importance of Newfoundland and Labrador–Québec
relations.

Rather, it may expand the context within which those relations operate.

5. The Largest Remaining Strategic Weakness: Lack of Independent Optionality

One of the strongest observations emerging from the work completed thus far is that optionality
may ultimately represent the central strategic issue.

Hydro-Québec appears to possess significantly greater optionality than Newfoundland and
Labrador.

The issue is significant because optionality influences BATNA — Best Alternative to a
Negotiated Agreement.

The stronger the alternatives available to a negotiating party, the stronger its bargaining position
tends to be.

Observations and recommendations arising from the IRC identified aspects of this issue. Similar
concerns emerged from analyses involving transmission developments, Hydro-Québec's broader
system integration, and the strategic role of interconnections.

The issue is important because optionality creates choices.

And choices influence bargaining leverage.

The analyses reviewed suggest Newfoundland and Labrador does not yet appear to possess a
fully articulated long-term strategy for strengthening independent optionality.

This does not necessarily imply complete independence from Hydro-Québec.

Nor does it imply that cooperation is undesirable.

Rather, it suggests that broader development options may strengthen Newfoundland and
Labrador's future strategic position.

This observation appears particularly important because many of the opportunities discussed
throughout this assessment ultimately depend upon the existence of meaningful strategic choices.

Without stronger optionality, Newfoundland and Labrador's bargaining position may remain
structurally weaker than that of Hydro-Québec.

6. What Remains Unresolved?

One of the observations emerging from this work is that a number of important strategic issues
surrounding Churchill River development have become more visible recently.

The work of the Independent Review Committee (IRC), together with the broader analyses
reviewed throughout this assessment, has contributed to bringing a number of important issues
forward.

This is important given the significance of the Churchill River resource to Newfoundland and
Labrador's future and what appears to be an evolving strategic environment surrounding
electricity development, transmission, and market participation.

However, identifying issues and developing the strategic capabilities required to address them
are not the same thing.

A number of important questions remain unresolved.

Others may continue to emerge as the strategic environment evolves.

7. Conclusion

Several conclusions emerge from this assessment.

First, the strategic environment surrounding Churchill River development appears to be
changing.

The analyses reviewed suggest increasing importance may be attached to transmission,
interconnection, flexibility, reliability, climate adaptation, and broader system integration.

Second, a number of important issues have been identified and brought forward for
consideration.

This is important given the significance of the Churchill River resource to Newfoundland and
Labrador's future and the possibility that the environment within which future decisions will be
made may itself be evolving.

Third, Hydro-Québec appears to be operating within a highly integrated strategic framework
linking long-term development objectives, transmission, reliability, market participation, and
system planning.

This may have important implications for Newfoundland and Labrador as it considers future
development opportunities and negotiations involving the Churchill River resource.

Finally, the central issue identified throughout this assessment is not whether negotiations should
occur.

Rather, it is whether the strategic environment surrounding Churchill River development may be
changing in important ways and what implications those changes may have for future
negotiations and development decisions.

If the observations discussed throughout this assessment are broadly correct, continued attention
to these developments is critical. Ensuring that emerging opportunities, risks, and strategic
considerations are recognized and incorporated into future planning, preparation, and
negotiations may ultimately prove as important as the negotiations themselves.

A companion paper, Strategic Preparation for Churchill River Development and Negotiations,
explores several ways Newfoundland and Labrador can strengthen its preparedness for future
negotiations and development decisions.
